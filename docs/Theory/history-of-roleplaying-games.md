# A History of Roleplaying Games

## Why this history matters

Roleplaying games are not a single mechanic. They are a family of ways to inhabit a situation, make decisions from within a role, and discover consequences under an agreed structure.

Across their history, the medium has changed repeatedly:

- A referee once interpreted a changing situation and applied rules.
- A tabletop group began treating individual characters as persistent participants in a shared world.
- Computer programs took over bookkeeping, random resolution, maps, combat, and eventually large portions of world simulation.
- Networked systems let many people inhabit one persistent environment.
- Modern games combine authored stories, systemic simulation, cinematic presentation, online services, user-generated content, and tabletop-style rules in different proportions.

The important question for SURPS is not “Which game is the one true RPG?” It is: **Which responsibilities belong to the participants, which belong to the rules, which belong to the world, and which can be delegated to a machine?**

This is a selective history of influential transitions, not an exhaustive catalogue. Dates identify important publications or releases; the ideas often developed earlier and in parallel.

## 1. Before the modern tabletop RPG: rules, referees, and roles

### Military and recreational wargames

Long before fantasy roleplaying, wargames explored how written rules could represent a situation too large or complex to resolve by moving real armies. The nineteenth-century Prussian *Kriegsspiel* tradition used maps, written orders, reports, adjudication, and referees. Players acted from a command role; a referee interpreted orders and resolved events that the rules or map could not settle mechanically.

Civilian miniatures wargaming later made military simulation a hobby. H. G. Wells's *Little Wars* (1913) helped popularize a recreational miniature battle game, while post-war commercial wargames and hobby communities developed more detailed rules, campaigns, magazines, and conventions.

These traditions contributed several durable ideas:

- A shared model of a world or battlefield.
- Participants who act from an assigned role and have incomplete information.
- Rules that constrain possible actions without scripting every decision.
- A referee or adjudicator who resolves ambiguity and hidden events.
- Persistent campaigns in which one session changes the next.

### Braunstein and individual agency

In the 1960s, designer David Wesely's *Braunstein* games experimented with participants playing individual people in a shared town rather than simply commanding units. The referee managed the situation while players pursued their own goals, negotiated, cooperated, and came into conflict. The scenario could evolve in ways the referee had not prewritten.

This was a major conceptual step: the player was no longer only an operator of a game piece. The player was making decisions as a person inside a situation.

### Chainmail, Blackmoor, and Dungeons & Dragons

Gary Gygax and Jeff Perren's *Chainmail* (1971) was a medieval miniatures wargame with a fantasy supplement. Dave Arneson's *Blackmoor* campaign combined fantasy adventure with persistent individual characters, exploration, and referee-led play. These strands converged in the first published *Dungeons & Dragons* in 1974, created by Gary Gygax and Dave Arneson.

D&D established a recognizable tabletop form:

- Each player controls a continuing character.
- A Dungeon Master (DM) describes the world, portrays other inhabitants, and adjudicates uncertain outcomes.
- Players state what their characters attempt.
- Rules, character capabilities, dice, and the DM resolve uncertainty.
- Characters and the campaign persist and change between sessions.

The first D&D rules were still closely connected to fantasy wargaming. The form quickly expanded beyond combat into exploration, problem-solving, negotiation, discovery, and collaborative storytelling. The game did not invent role assumption or all of its component ideas; it brought them together in a publishable, extensible game form.

**SURPS connection:** separate the entity (the character), the participant's intent, the situation, the rule used to resolve uncertainty, the adjudicator/provider, and the resulting state change. These are related, but they are not the same thing.

## 2. Tabletop systems diversify: what should rules describe?

The late 1970s and 1980s saw many systems explore different answers to the question of what an RPG should model.

Examples include:

- *Tunnels & Trolls* (1975), which offered a different and more approachable fantasy rules structure.
- *RuneQuest* (1978), with a skill-oriented approach and a deeply integrated setting.
- *Traveller* (1977), which brought science-fiction careers, travel, trade, and danger into tabletop roleplaying.
- *Call of Cthulhu* (1981), emphasizing investigation, horror, vulnerability, and psychological consequences.
- *GURPS* (1986), which pursued a generic toolkit intended to support many genres and settings.
- *Cyberpunk* (1988), which developed a distinct technological and social genre identity.

These titles are not interchangeable. They demonstrate that the roleplaying form can support different assumptions about character growth, danger, probability, skills, narrative authority, setting, and campaign structure.

The hobby also developed multiple play cultures: tactical combat, character immersion, investigation, emergent sandbox campaigns, dramatic narrative, live action roleplaying, and rules-light improvisation. These approaches overlap; none is the whole history.

**SURPS connection:** a universal foundation should not force one genre's assumptions into every experience. Shared concepts can coexist with optional rulebooks, setting conventions, and different levels of detail.

## 3. The first computer RPGs: automating the referee's ledger

Computer roleplaying emerged from more than one influence. Tabletop D&D was important, but computer adventure, academic computing, wargames, sports simulations, and early interactive fiction also contributed.

During the 1970s, university and mainframe environments hosted early dungeon and adventure programs. Their exact ancestry is sometimes disputed because programs were copied, renamed, and independently recreated across institutions. It is more accurate to describe a cluster of experiments than to declare one uncontested first computer RPG.

The personal-computer era made the form more visible:

- *Akalabeth: World of Doom* (1980) and the *Ultima* series (beginning 1981) explored characters moving through a persistent fantasy world, with towns, wilderness, dungeons, equipment, and progression.
- *Wizardry: Proving Grounds of the Mad Overlord* (1981) emphasized a party, first-person dungeon exploration, character creation, statistics, and tactical encounters.
- *Rogue* (1980) helped establish procedural dungeon exploration, resource pressure, and permadeath conventions later associated with roguelikes.
- *The Bard's Tale* (1985) brought party-based dungeon crawling to a broad home-computer audience.

The computer could now perform tasks that a tabletop referee or player group had previously handled manually:

- Store character sheets and inventories.
- Roll random numbers and apply tables.
- Track hit points, experience, equipment, and conditions.
- Enforce turn order and action legality.
- Generate or display maps and encounters.
- Save and restore campaign state.

But the trade-off was immediate. A computer could only resolve what its program represented. The human referee could accept an unusual plan, ask a clarifying question, invent a consequence, or reinterpret a rule. Early computer RPGs generally exposed a narrower vocabulary of commands and interactions.

**SURPS connection:** digital automation should be treated as an implementation of explicit rules and providers, not as permission to hide rules inside a title-specific script. The interface may constrain what can be attempted; the underlying model should make those constraints inspectable.

## 4. Console RPGs: translating the form for a different audience and rhythm

Console games developed their own conventions, influenced by computer RPGs but adapted to controllers, television screens, limited memory, and a different play context.

- *Dragon Quest* (1986, Japan) made a clear, approachable console RPG structure from exploration, towns, equipment, turn-based combat, and character growth.
- *Final Fantasy* (1987) developed party composition, jobs, magic, and a large authored adventure.
- Later series entries experimented with character-driven stories, tactical systems, real-time elements, and cinematic presentation.

These games often made the world more authored and the available interactions more explicit. A player might choose from menus rather than describe any imaginable action. This is not a failure of roleplaying; it is a different contract between player and simulation.

A console RPG can deliver carefully paced progression, music, animation, authored scenes, and readable tactical choices. It can also make the boundaries of the simulation less visible: if a character cannot interact with an object, the game may simply provide no command for it.

**SURPS connection:** distinguish player-facing affordances from world capabilities. A menu is one way to expose valid actions; it should not be mistaken for the complete ontology of what an entity or object is capable of.

## 5. Tactical and computer-assisted tabletop play

The 1980s and 1990s also saw computer games adapt tabletop rules more directly.

- SSI's *Gold Box* games, beginning with *Pool of Radiance* (1988), translated Advanced Dungeons & Dragons party statistics, tactical movement, spells, and turn-based combat into a computer-managed encounter.
- *Baldur's Gate* (1998) brought a party-based D&D computer RPG to a real-time-with-pause format.
- *Planescape: Torment* (1999) demonstrated how dialogue, identity, memory, and philosophical choices could become central to a computer RPG.
- *Neverwinter Nights* (2002) combined a D&D-based game with tools and multiplayer persistent-world possibilities.

These titles show several different digitization strategies:

1. **Rules fidelity:** encode a tabletop ruleset closely enough that the computer can adjudicate it.
2. **Selective automation:** automate arithmetic and tactical resolution while keeping narrative choice authored or player-directed.
3. **Real-time-with-pause:** let the simulation advance continuously, but allow players to pause and issue coordinated decisions.
4. **Tooling and extensibility:** expose editors, scripting, or multiplayer tools so communities can build experiences beyond the shipped campaign.

A digital adaptation is always an interpretation. It chooses which tabletop rules to preserve, simplify, omit, or replace.

## 6. Persistent multiplayer worlds: the environment becomes shared infrastructure

Text-based multi-user dungeons (MUDs) developed in the late 1970s and early 1980s. They allowed multiple participants to explore and interact in a shared networked environment. Their worlds combined roleplaying, social interaction, puzzles, combat, and persistent spaces in different proportions.

Graphical online worlds later expanded the scale and presentation:

- *Meridian 59* (1996) and *Ultima Online* (1997) helped establish commercial graphical online roleplaying worlds.
- *EverQuest* (1999) developed persistent party-based exploration, character advancement, and large-scale cooperative encounters.
- *World of Warcraft* (2004) brought many MMORPG conventions to a very large audience through accessible questing, instanced encounters, social guilds, and persistent progression.

Networked roleplaying changes the system boundary. The world is no longer merely stored in one player's save file. It becomes a service coordinating identity, time, shared state, authority, persistence, and synchronization across participants.

This introduces new design responsibilities:

- Who owns authoritative state?
- What happens when players disagree about an event?
- How are simultaneous actions ordered?
- What is persistent, and what is temporary?
- How does the system handle latency, disconnection, cheating, and moderation?
- Which rules are shared globally, and which belong to a particular world or instance?

**SURPS connection:** a roleplaying ontology should describe the world and its rules without assuming that one client or one local process is authoritative. Execution, persistence, networking, and presentation are separate responsibilities.

## 7. Action RPGs, immersive simulations, and systemic worlds

Not every RPG uses turns or a party. Some titles place the player directly inside continuous action, with character capabilities expressed through timing, movement, equipment, and build progression.

- *The Legend of Zelda* (1986) helped popularize real-time exploration and action in an adventure structure; its classification as an RPG varies by genre convention.
- *Diablo* (1996) emphasized real-time combat, randomized loot, character builds, and repeatable progression.
- *The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind* (2002) and *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim* (2011) offered open-world exploration, character development, factions, quests, and broad player-directed activity.
- *Deus Ex* (2000) and *System Shock 2* (1999) blended roleplaying progression with immersive simulation, environmental interaction, and multiple approaches to problems.

These games demonstrate that roleplaying can emerge from interacting systems, not only from dialogue choices or a formal turn structure. An action can depend on position, timing, equipment, skill, environment, and the state of other entities.

A crucial design distinction appears here: an authored animation is not the same as a general action model. A sword swing animation may look convincing, but a reusable sword interaction must also account for reach, orientation, contact, timing, balance, training, interruption, and consequences.

**SURPS connection:** this is where Gestures and Physics become important partners. The object or environment can offer an interaction provider; the agent contributes capability, anatomy, training, condition, intent, and timing; Physics determines physical consequences. The resulting behavior should not be reduced to a guaranteed cinematic outcome.

## 8. Narrative choice and authored consequence

Some computer RPGs emphasize dialogue, relationships, moral choices, and branching authored outcomes:

- *Ultima IV* (1985) made virtues and ethical conduct central to progression.
- *Baldur's Gate* (1998) and *Baldur's Gate II* (2000) combined party interaction, dialogue, exploration, and tactical combat.
- *The Witcher* series (beginning 2007) explored consequential choices and morally complicated authored quests.
- *Mass Effect* (2007) made dialogue selection, relationships, and cross-game narrative continuity highly visible.

These systems often represent choice as a set of authored branches, flags, variables, and consequences. That can be powerful and emotionally effective, but it differs from open-ended referee adjudication. A choice can only produce a distinct result if the designers authored or generated one.

**SURPS connection:** StoryBuilder should own narrative construction; SURPS should describe entities, intent, state, rules, and consequences that narrative systems can observe. The story layer should not secretly replace physical or capability rules.

## 9. User-generated worlds, survival, and simulation sandboxes

Later games increasingly blurred the line between RPG, simulation, construction, survival, and social world.

- *Minecraft* (2009) foregrounded a manipulable world and player construction; it is not always classified as an RPG, but it demonstrates how persistent world state and player-created goals can support role-like play.
- *Mount & Blade* (2008) combined character progression, tactical command, and large-scale real-time combat.
- *Kenshi* (2018) combined open-ended survival, squad control, emergent conflict, and a world that does not revolve around a conventional authored protagonist.
- *Baldur's Gate 3* (2023) demonstrated a modern high-production adaptation of tabletop-style turn-based encounters, party agency, and branching narrative.

The lesson is not that every sandbox is an RPG. It is that roleplaying ideas increasingly appear in systems whose primary labels may be survival, strategy, simulation, construction, or action.

**SURPS connection:** a universal model should be reusable outside a game marketed as an RPG. An agent, object, environment, capability, and consequence do not become different kinds of things merely because the experience is called a simulator, workshop, city, or story.

## 10. What digitization changed — and what it did not

Digital systems have progressively automated or expanded:

| Tabletop responsibility | Common digital expression | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Character sheet | Persistent entity data | Stored fields do not automatically explain meaning or applicability |
| Dice and tables | Random number generation and resolution code | Randomness does not guarantee fair or transparent rules |
| Game master adjudication | Scripts, rule engines, AI agents, or human moderators | A script only handles cases it knows how to represent |
| Map and minis | Tile maps, 3D scenes, physics worlds | Visual geometry is not the same as semantic world knowledge |
| Initiative and turns | Turn queues, clocks, real-time simulation, pause | Timing and concurrency become implementation concerns |
| Character advancement | XP, levels, skills, unlocks, build systems | Progression can be disconnected from actual capability |
| Campaign memory | Save files, databases, server state | Persistence introduces authority, versioning, and migration concerns |
| Shared imagination | Dialogue, animation, procedural content, user tools | Authored content can narrow the space of possible actions |

Computers are exceptionally good at consistency, repetition, arithmetic, storage, visualization, and executing explicit procedures. Humans remain capable of interpreting ambiguity, negotiating meaning, inventing unexpected possibilities, and changing the social contract at the table.

A good digital system does not need to imitate every human activity. It should make clear which parts are automated, which remain human decisions, and which are outside the model.

## 11. A SURPS synthesis

SURPS takes the historical record as a set of lessons rather than a template to copy.

1. **Keep the tabletop game complete.** The rules must be understandable and playable with people, paper, pencil, and agreed randomization. A runtime is an implementation, not the source of truth.
2. **Separate the shared world from a particular presentation.** A tabletop referee, a console menu, a 3D scene, and a networked server can expose the same underlying concepts differently.
3. **Make rules inspectable.** Participants and editors should be able to identify the rulebook, provider, assumptions, inputs, outputs, and evidence behind a resolution.
4. **Allow multiple levels of fidelity.** A quick abstract check and a detailed physical interaction may describe the same event at different behavioral LODs. The selected source must be explicit.
5. **Treat time as part of the model.** Paused turns, initiative, reaction windows, continuous action, interruption, and recovery are different policies over time—not merely animation settings.
6. **Let the interacted-with world contribute behavior.** A ladder offers climbing interactions; a sword offers weapon-use interactions. An agent's anatomy, skill, condition, and intent determine which attempts are available and how they proceed.
7. **Keep domain ownership clear.** Physics describes physical consequences; Gestures describes embodied action and motion; StoryBuilder constructs narrative; the experience chooses what to compose.
8. **Make SURPS optional at the experience boundary.** An experience that does not enable SURPS should not need SURPS discovery or rulebook dependencies.

The long arc is from people interpreting a shared situation together, to software taking over selected responsibilities, to systems that can expose reusable world behavior across many kinds of experiences. SURPS aims to make that last step explicit without discarding the human-readable rules that came before it.
