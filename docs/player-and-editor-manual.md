# SURPS Player and Editor Manual

SURPS is a roleplaying system before it is a software feature. The tabletop rules must stand on their own: a group should be able to create a world, choose sourcebooks, play characters, resolve uncertainty, and record consequences with paper, pencil, dice or another agreed method, and conversation. No app, internet connection, account, registry, or digital runtime is required.

This manual has two equal responsibilities: teach people how to play SURPS at a table, and explain how a digital experience can automate the same procedures without changing their meaning.

## Part I — The tabletop roleplaying system

### 1. What a roleplaying game is

Explain players, game master/referee, characters, entities, setting, intent, interaction, uncertainty, consequences, and shared authorship. Establish the table's social contract, authority, safety tools, and how rulings are recorded.

### 2. Creating a character or agent

Describe identity, attributes, capabilities, training, anatomy, condition, equipment, goals, relationships, and history. Separate what a character is from what it can currently do. Provide printable character records and explain every field.

### 3. Creating a world and choosing rulebooks

Start with SURPS CORE. The group then chooses the sourcebooks that apply to its campaign, genre, era, domains, and desired detail. LOD labels communicate the depth/specialization of a book; they do not automatically select or override it. Record the chosen books and any permitted optional procedures on a campaign sheet.

### 4. Declaring intent and resolving interactions

Teach the player-facing procedure: describe intent, identify target/context, consult the selected rulebook/provider, establish applicable capabilities and constraints, resolve uncertainty, describe the outcome, and update the character/world record. Explain when the referee makes a ruling and how to keep it consistent.

### 5. Time, turns, initiative, and reaction

Explain turn boundaries, decision windows, delta time, action phases, initiative, reaction opportunities, interruption, deadlines, recovery, and what happens when a player does not decide in time. Include both paused turn-based play and continuously advancing encounters. Give table-ready procedures that do not depend on a digital clock.

### 6. Conflict and capability

Explain opposed capabilities, trained/untrained behavior, equipment providers, risk, injury, recovery, and consequences without assuming digital animation or automation. Include examples for physical, social, investigative, and environmental conflict.

### 7. Running an offline session

Provide the complete paper procedure, character and entity records, campaign rulebook list, referee worksheets, timing aids, dice/tables where defined, examples, and glossary. A reader must be able to play without an application, internet connection, or digital registry.

## Part II — Rulebook families and worked campaigns

### 8. CORE and optional sourcebooks

Explain the shared baseline, declared scope, optional detail, specialization, alternatives, and conflict resolution. Teach the group to state which book governs which subject or procedure. Never assume that a larger LOD number automatically wins.

### 9. Worked campaign: spying across settings

Use the same core concept—spying—to show four compatible ways to prepare a campaign:

- CORE spying: universal concepts and a playable baseline procedure.
- LOD 1 Spying Through History: historical tradecraft and broad changes across eras.
- LOD 2 Cold War Espionage: a specific historical campaign framework.
- LOD 3 Cyberpunk Espionage: a speculative campaign framework with different technology and institutions.

The Cold War and cyberpunk books are alternative campaign selections, not successive mandatory upgrades. A group can use CORE alone, add selected LOD 1 material, or choose one setting book. Explain how the referee records scope and resolves any actual conflict.

## Part III — How SURPS is digitized

### 10. From tabletop procedure to explicit model

Map players, game master, characters, attributes, capabilities, actions, timing, randomization, state, and consequences to digital concepts. Explain that digital execution must preserve the tabletop rules rather than inventing hidden rules.

### 11. Opt-in composition

Show SURPS as an experience setting: disabled means no SURPS rulebook system is required; enabled composes the master system and selected rulebooks. Explain how an experience can use FSM_API/FSM_COS/FSM_Foundry without SURPS.

### 12. Rulebook registration and provider discovery

Explain stable identity/version, registration, applicability, dependencies, provenance, conflict handling, editor visibility, and deterministic resolution. Explain the tabletop equivalent: the campaign's selected books and recorded rulings.

### 13. Gesture providers and embodied behavior

Explain how objects and environments offer interaction providers, while agents contribute anatomy, skill, condition, intent, and time. Include ladder climbing, sword use, trained/untrained behavior, injury transitions, and failure alternatives. Provide manual procedures alongside any digital evaluator.

### 14. Physics and mathematical binding

Explain typed quantities, units, dimensions, assumptions, physical constraints, domain-owned evaluators, and evidence. Clearly distinguish implemented provider capability from conceptual rules. Give a manual resolution method where a rule is foundational to play.

### 15. LOD, determinism, and execution

Explain selected rule source, declared scope, explicit precedence/fallback, delta-time stepping, state transitions, reproducibility, and editor diagnostics. Numeric LOD alone is never an implicit conflict resolver.

### 16. Editor handbook

Document how to enable SURPS, choose rulebooks/LOD by scope, inspect descriptors and provenance, diagnose missing providers/inputs, and verify behavior without silently changing source rules. Include a printable campaign configuration sheet and a digital equivalent.

## Editorial contract

Every chapter should contain:

1. Theory and vocabulary.
2. Tabletop/offline procedure.
3. A worked example.
4. Player and referee guidance.
5. A digital mapping, where applicable.
6. A printable reference or worksheet where it helps.
7. Links to authoritative domain implementations and evidence.

This is the chapter plan, not a claim that all chapters or digital providers are already implemented. A chapter is not complete merely because its digital API exists; its human procedure and examples must also be usable.
